How this guide is written

This is an introductory educational library from Snowman’s Apiary, not a complete diagnostic manual. Articles summarize extension and government resources in original language, with links beside the relevant sections. Our apiary’s personal story and reported treatment practice are identified separately.

Using regional sources

General colony biology applies across regions, but bloom dates and management calendars do not transfer automatically. Pennsylvania and Mississippi seasonal resources explain principles; the local notes adapt those ideas to questions to watch in southwest Kansas. Oklahoma forage examples are regional starting points to investigate, not promises of a Kansas bloom or harvest.

When advice changes

Sources were checked October 20, 2026. Product labels, approved uses and local requirements can change. Read the current label on the product you intend to use, and seek local diagnostic help when a colony is unwell.

Science behind the fact banner

The banner changes on each page visit or when you select “Another fact.” It does not rotate while you read. The flower-electricity studies distinguish bumblebees from honey bees; they do not establish that honey bees outperform other pollinators.

A tiny electrical world
Honey bees accumulate electric charge as they fly. Experiments show that electric fields can move their antennae and that bees can learn to associate those signals with a reward. Greggers and colleagues · 2013
Flowers have electrical cues
Bumblebees can learn differences in floral electric fields. A visit from a charged bee changes those cues—one more piece of information alongside a flower’s color, scent and shape. Clarke and colleagues · Science, 2013
Hairs that sense a field
In bumblebees, tiny body hairs move in response to weak electric fields. Researchers found these hairs can help turn an electrical cue into a sensory signal. University of Bristol · 2016
A different kind of family tree
A honey bee queen usually produces daughters from fertilized eggs and sons from unfertilized eggs. Female bees have two chromosome sets; male drones have one. Penn State Extension · Honey Bee Genetics Basics
One queen, many fathers
A queen mates with multiple drones, so workers in the same honey bee colony can have different fathers. A hive is a family with a surprisingly complex genetic story. Penn State Extension · Honey Bee Genetics Basics
Royal beginnings
Female honey bee larvae can become queens or workers. The rich, sustained royal-jelly diet given to queen-destined larvae helps shape that remarkable difference. Penn State Extension · Queen Honey Bee Development
A hive with air conditioning
Honey bees use water and wing-fanning to help cool their nest. A dependable water supply matters during hot weather. Oregon State Extension · Bees in a heat wave
Winter is not a long nap
Honey bees form a heat-producing winter cluster and use stored food as fuel. The colony needs access to that food throughout cold weather. Penn State Extension · Seasonal management
Nectar and pollen do different jobs
Nectar provides carbohydrate energy, while pollen supplies nutrients for growing bees. Colony nutrition takes more than honey alone. Mississippi State Extension · Colony growth
Honey bees are part of a bigger story
The western honey bee is not native to the Americas. Native bees were pollinating here long before honey bee colonies arrived with European colonists. USDA ARS · History of U.S. beekeeping
