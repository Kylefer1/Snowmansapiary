An illustrated year on the High Plains

Seasons are broad guides, not fixed treatment or harvest dates. Your selected chapter appears first. All four remain available below.

Spring · Growth · forage · swarm awareness

Build up, with care.

Brood rearing increases as resources return. A run of warm days can be followed by a freeze; growing brood makes food demand rise even when bees cannot fly.

What the beekeeper watches

  • Check food reserves and signs of queen activity during suitable inspection weather.
  • Give expanding colonies appropriate space; learn to recognize swarm preparation.
  • Begin or continue mite monitoring; record the result for each hive.

Local lens: watch actual blooms and the forecast around Liberal. A warm afternoon does not guarantee a dependable nectar flow.

Summer · Water · nectar · heat

Follow the flowers.

The colony gathers food and may build a harvestable surplus. Heat and drought can interrupt forage. A large, busy colony still needs a mite assessment.

What the beekeeper watches

  • Keep a dependable clean water source with safe landing places.
  • Assess stores during dry spells; harvest only a genuine surplus.
  • Watch for robbing during a dearth; keep inspections efficient.

Local lens: track rainfall, mowing and nearby bloom. A flowering field may offer a short opportunity, and dry weather can change the picture quickly.

Autumn · Winter bees · stores · mite follow-up

Next spring starts now.

As growth slows, the colony prepares for winter. Healthy long-lived workers and accessible food reserves matter more than a last small honey harvest.

What the beekeeper watches

  • Confirm mite control worked; do not assume a completed treatment means low mites.
  • Assess food and colony strength early enough to address shortages.
  • Protect equipment from weather and reduce opportunities for robbing and pests.

Local lens: treat a fall nectar flow as something to verify. Prepare for cold spells using measured stores and colony condition.

Winter · Shelter · food access · quiet checks

A living cluster.

Honey bees stay active within a heat-producing cluster. Stored food fuels that work. Food elsewhere in the hive may be hard to reach during prolonged cold.

What the beekeeper watches

  • Check hive security and entrances from outside; avoid needless disruption.
  • Monitor remaining food and use locally appropriate emergency feeding if needed.
  • Review records and plan equipment and learning for the next season.

Local lens: winter sunshine can bring activity, followed by another cold spell. Avoid treating a brief flight window as proof that winter is over.

Why southwest Kansas changes the plan

Use the National Weather Service’s southwest Kansas observations, Liberal climate normals and drought resources to put the year in context. Climate averages describe patterns; they do not predict this week’s forage or replace an inspection.

Drought connects water, flowers and food

Dry conditions can reduce both forage and water availability. Watch colony stores even in summer, and keep supplemental feed separate from honey intended for sale. When feeding is needed, choose a method suited to weather and colony needs.

Keep a bloom diary

Write down the plant, first bloom, bee visits, rainfall and changes in hive stores. Regional examples to investigate include sweetclover, alfalfa and sunflowers. Their value depends on location, weather and land management; Oklahoma bloom dates should not be copied as a Liberal calendar. These examples do not identify the floral source of Snowman’s honey.
